# What Is Language? A Tutorial on Symbols, Meaning, and the Mind

> **Core thesis:** Language is a symbolic, context-dependent reconstruction protocol. A mind encodes concepts into forms; another mind decodes those forms into meaning. Language does not transmit reality directly — it transmits symbols that let us reconstruct a conceptualized reality.

This tutorial builds that thesis from the ground up. By the end, you will understand what language is, where it comes from, how it is structured, how it encodes meaning, why it is ambiguous, and why building machines that truly understand language remains an open research problem.

## 1. What Is Language?

Ask a room full of people "What is language?" and you will get a spread of answers. Here is a representative set:

- A mode of communication.
- A structural way to represent ideas so others can understand them.
- A linguistic mode of communication in the form of text, voice, or body.
- A structured arrangement of words in grammatical order to communicate thought.
- A set of words with grammatical rules.
- A string of symbols for thoughts.
- A defined script or system.

Each answer captures part of the truth. Synthesizing them:

> **Language is a structured symbolic system used to express, organize, and reconstruct thoughts and concepts between minds.**

### A simple example

You feel thirsty. The full pipeline looks like this:

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Idea / concept** | "I need water." |
| **Form / symbol** | Say "water," point to a bottle, send a 💧 emoji, or write "water." |
| **Listener decodes** | Understands your inner idea. |

That is the entire course in miniature: a concept is encoded into a form, transmitted, and decoded back into a concept.

## 2. Where Language Comes From: The Mind as Source

Language originates in the mind. The process unfolds in four steps:

```
Idea  →  Conceptualization  →  Articulation  →  Medium
```

1. **Idea** — something arises in your mind.
2. **Conceptualization** — you shape it into a definite concept: "this is the idea."
3. **Articulation** — you decide how to say it.
4. **Medium** — you need a channel (speech, writing, gesture) to express it.

Language is therefore a **window to the mind**. It is the sequence of thoughts that helps you generalize your concepts, ideas, and messages.

### Language organizes thought

Sit still, close your eyes, and simply observe your thoughts for a minute. You will notice ideas popping up randomly — messages, fragments, associations — all completely disorganized.

Think of a **messy room**: nobody wants to be in it because nothing can be found. Tidy the room, and it becomes a place you want to spend time in. Language does the same for thought:

> **Unstructured thought is noise; structured thought is meaning. Language is the structuring tool.**

When you organize and structure your thoughts, you add meaning to them. This is one of language's most important functions — not just expressing thought, but *shaping* it.

### Language and the cognitive system

Language also helps us study our own cognitive system. Humans have **five perception systems**:

1. **Vision** — signals through the eyes
2. **Hearing** — signals through the ears
3. **Touch** — signals through the skin
4. **Taste** — signals through the tongue
5. **Smell (odor)** — signals through the nose

And speech, which coordinates listening and speaking. Studying language helps us understand how these perceptual and cognitive systems interact.

**Why study language at all?** It helps you describe the systematicity, structure, and function of language — and how those functions are realized by the language system.

## 3. The Five Subsystems of Language

Language is divided into multiple subsystems. The cleanest way to see them is to take one sentence and climb the ladder:

> **"The cats jumped over the wall."**

```
Sounds  →  Words  →  Sentences  →  Meaning  →  Use/Context
```

| Level | Subsystem | What it studies | Example from the sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Phonetics / Phonology** | Sounds and syllables | /kæt/, /dʒʌmp/ |
| 2 | **Morphology** | Structure of words | cat + s, jump + ed |
| 3 | **Syntax** | Sentence structure, phrases, clauses | Subject + verb + prepositional phrase |
| 4 | **Semantics** | Meaning | Cats moved over a wall |
| 5 | **Pragmatics** | Use in context | Why say it? What does "over" mean here? |

### How the levels connect

- **Phonetics/phonology** deals with sounds and syllables.
- **Morphology** deals with how sounds combine into words and the structure of those words.
- **Syntax** deals with how words combine into phrases and sentences.
- **Semantics** deals with the meaning that emerges once structure exists. As with organizing your thoughts, organizing words and clauses carves out meaning.
- **Pragmatics** deals with *when* and *how* to use a particular meaning or structure — the functionalist approach to language. People say "be pragmatic" or "be practical" in daily life; pragmatics is the linguistic version of that: knowing when to use which meaning, under what circumstances.

### Pragmatics in action: the word "bank"

> "I sat near the bank."

What does this mean?

- If you are financially inclined, you picture a **financial institution**.
- If you are a nature person, you picture the **bank of the Ganga river**.

The word alone is not enough. **Context** decides. Providing context lets the listener understand the meaning you originally intended. Discourse information, context information, and the coherence of sentences all fall under **language use**, studied in pragmatics.

### Pragmatics in action: discourse coherence

Consider two Hindi sentences that mean almost the same thing:

- *Bharat ke pradhan mantri Narendra Modi hain.* ("India's prime minister is Narendra Modi.")
- *Narendra Modi hain.* ("It is Narendra Modi.")

If the prior discourse is about India — "India is a great nation, India has many states, India's prime minister is Narendra Modi" — then the first sentence feels natural. The second, in that context, feels odd: it is not what the coherence of the story leads you to expect.

These variations exist in every language — Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada, English, Hindi. Pragmatics studies them.

### The hierarchy as a circle

The same five-level hierarchy can be drawn as a circular plot: sounds feed words, words feed sentences, sentences feed meaning, meaning feeds use — and use feeds back into which sounds and structures we choose next.

## 4. What Do We Use Language For?

The obvious answer is **communication** — but push further. What activities?

- Talking to friends
- Communicating ideas
- Shopping (expressing what you want to buy)
- Negotiating
- Phone calls
- Interacting with web pages (a form of communication where you are not directly talking but exchanging messages)

### The "Let's meet tomorrow" example

> **"Let's meet tomorrow."**

This is crisp and short, yet highly effective. It communicates two things:

1. **Urgency** — not next year, not next month: *tomorrow*.
2. **Assertiveness** — it is direct and effective.

Language lets you express ideas clearly and in a more articulate way, making communication effective.

### The trap: taking language for granted

All of this is fine — but we **take language for granted**. Because our lives are surrounded by language, we assume we know all about it. We do not see how complex it is because we never look. The goal of studying language is to **peel apart its intricacies and complexities**.

## 5. Communication Without Language

If language is one channel, what are the others?

- **Sign language**
- **Written / text communication**
- **Visual communication**
- **Pictorial communication**
- **Morse code** — a coded form of communication
- **Signals and gestures**
- **Bluetooth** — two mobile phones communicating without language
- **Brain signaling** (speculative)
- **Telepathy** (not established, but often mentioned)

Some of these are still language-like — Morse code, for instance, is a coded form of language. And sign languages are full languages with their own grammar. The interesting question is what communication can happen **without** language at all. Bluetooth is a clean example: two devices exchange data with no language involved.

## 6. The Symbolic Function of Language

Communication is implemented via two approaches: **symbolic** and **interactive**. This section covers the symbolic one.

> **Language is symbolic in nature.**

The mechanism:

```
Concept appears in mind  →  You label it  →  The label is a symbol
```

When you label a concept, you symbolize it.

### Examples

| Concept | Symbol |
|---|---|
| Tree | The word "tree" |
| Justice | The word "justice" |
| Freedom | The word "freedom" |

Even very abstract ideas get words, so you can symbolize them and combine them into more complex ideas.

### Symbols need not be physical

A lion need not be in front of you for you to talk about a lion. You have the idea of a lion, so you can say: "Yesterday I saw a leopard on campus." (Leopards, unlike lions, do occasionally appear on campuses — but the point stands.) The physical referent is absent; the symbol still works.

### What counts as a symbol?

Alphabets, characters, numbers, words, phrases, constituents — all of these are symbols.

**Morphology example: "distaste"**

- *Distaste* means dislike.
- *Dis-* is a **prefix** meaning "opposite" or "not."
- The word contains a **root** (*taste*) plus a **prefix** (*dis-*).
- Prefixes and suffixes attach to root words to build meaning.

The idea of "opposite of liking" is represented by the symbol *dis-*.

**Sentence-level example**

> "If it rains tomorrow, I will not go to class."

This is a logical proposition — an if–else structure. The entire proposition is encoded symbolically in language.

### Forms: the clay analogy

Symbols are **forms**. Think of a lump of clay: you can shape it into many different forms — a toy, a bowl, a figure. Likewise, symbols can take different forms to express different ideas.

Forms can be:

- **Spoken**
- **Written**
- **Signed**

All forms must be **meaningful** — otherwise, why use them? This leads to the next core concept.

## 7. Form–Meaning Pairing

> **Every form must be paired with meaning. This is form–meaning pairing — a very important characteristic of language.**

```
FORM  (symbol: sound, spelling, gesture)
  ↕
MEANING  (concept: what the mind understands)
```

Examples of forms:

- The spoken sound "cat"
- The written letters c-a-t
- A phonetic transcription of "cat"
- The dollar sign **$**
- A picture of a cat

Each of these is a form that triggers a meaning in the mind of someone who has learned the pairing.

**Why the dollar sign works:** When you see **$**, "money" or "US dollar" comes to mind. Why? Because the symbol has been **labeled** in your mind through training and experience. It is just a symbol, but it activates a stored conceptual representation. The same is true of the rupee sign (₹).

## 8. What Is Meaning?

Meaning is associated with **understanding**. Anything your mind can understand is meaningful.

The process:

```
Idea  →  choose a symbol to express it  →  another person acknowledges the idea through that symbol  →  the symbol has meaning
```

Meaning is a kind of **ideation** — an idea that is **conventionally accepted**, something your mind understands. Understanding and meaning are associated concepts.

### The "cat" example with phonetic transcription

Suppose you see the word "cat" written in **phonetic transcription** — symbols that induce the sound /kæt/ in your mind. Just as a picture of a cat induces the concept of a cat, phonetic symbols induce the sound, which activates the concept.

This is what dictionaries do: they give you pronunciation (phonetic transcription), meaning, and example sentences showing how to use the word.

### The "pear" vs. "pier" example

English has two words with the same pronunciation: *pear* (the fruit) and *pier* (a structure extending into water). If someone just says the sound, context and spelling disambiguate. This shows that form alone is not always enough — meaning depends on more than sound.

## 9. Inner World and Outer World

Language represents our **conceptualized experience** of the world — not the world directly.

Divide your world into two:

| World | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Inner world** | Your mental reality — what is in your mind |
| **Outer world** | Whatever you interact with — the external environment |

**You need language to interact with the outer world.**

### The cat image walkthrough

Suppose you see a picture of a cat.

1. **Outer world:** the picture of the cat.
2. **Perception:** your eyes receive shape, texture, color, skin, structure — all the properties.
3. **Reconstruction:** your mind integrates this perceptual information into a single mental image.
4. **Concept matching:** you match the reconstructed image against an existing concept — "cat" — that you have built over time.
5. **Meaning:** if it matches, you build meaning.
6. **Form:** you express that meaning using the symbol "cat."

```
OUTER WORLD (cat image)
      ↓  eyes / perception
PERCEPTS (shape, texture, color…)
      ↓  reconstruction
CONCEPT ("cat")
      ↕  matching (double arrow)
MEANING
      ↓
FORM ("cat")
```

Notice the **double arrow** between concept and meaning: they interact. The linguistic meaning emerges from the match between the perceived concept and the stored concept.

### The mind corrects errors

If the spelling of "cat" is wrong, or the image is ambiguous between cat and dog, your mind **corrects** it. This is why you can read paragraphs with jumbled or misspelled words — your mind reconstructs the intended meaning. The image and the phonetic transcription together trigger the concept.

### No physical cat required

There is no physical cat present. The word "cat" is **not the actual animal**. It is a **symbolic representation** that allows us to mentally access or activate the concept of a cat even when no cat is physically present.

## 10. Perception vs. Concept vs. Meaning

This distinction is subtle but essential. Use the **pear** example.

1. **Perception:** Different parts of the brain perceive the pear's shape, color, texture, taste, smell, and other properties. These are **perceptual signals** — the percept.
2. **Concept:** You reconstruct all the perceptual signals into an image/information and develop the **concept** "pear."
3. **Meaning:** You associate the concept with **meaning**.

> **Perception = signals. Concept = reconstructed mental representation. Meaning = understood concept paired with a form.**

Without a concept to link perception to meaning, you can perceive many things but not understand them — it becomes **noise**.

### The fear example

Suppose you see a dangerous animal and feel afraid. You have developed the concept of **fear**.

Now suppose you are forbidden from using the words *fear*, *scare*, or *afraid* — in any language. What forms can you use?

- **Gestures** — a fearful face, a recoiling body
- **Symbols** — a skull image
- **Colors** — red
- **Distance** — standing far away from the thing you fear

The point: **one can use different forms to express a linguistic meaning that encodes various concepts.** The concept of fear does not require the word "fear."

### A note on learning

When a baby sees a pear for the first time, it is learning the concept from percepts — being taught "this is a pear, this is green, this is how it looks, tastes, smells." An adult who already knows the concept perceives the image and immediately says "pear." The adult has **taken language for granted**. The goal of studying language is to **demystify** this — to peel apart the mechanisms of form–meaning pairing, concept-building, and inner–outer world interaction that we normally never notice.

## 11. Language Reconstructs Reality: Projected Reality

> **Language does not transmit reality. It reconstructs reality.**

What it transmits is **symbols** that allow us to reconstruct **conceptualized reality**. This is called **projected reality**.

```
World out there
      ↓  diverse perceptual signals
Integration into a single mental image
      ↓
Concept
      ↓  + meaning
Form / symbol
```

The world out there sends a diverse range of perceptual information. Your mind integrates it into a single image — a representation of your consciousness. That gives rise to the concept. The concept, paired with meaning, is expressed as a form.

**This is why language does not transmit reality directly:**
- The word "cat" is not a cat.
- The word "leopard" works even when no leopard is present.
- The symbol **$** is not money, but it activates the concept of money.

## 12. Encoding Different Realities Through Grammar

Language offers different grammatical tools to encode different types of reality.

| Type of reality | Example | Linguistic form |
|---|---|---|
| **Present** | "The cat **is sleeping**." | Present continuous (*is* + *-ing*) |
| **Past** | "The cat **was sleeping**." | Past tense (*was*) |
| **Imagined** | "The flying banana," "the cat jumping over the moon." | Open-ended vocabulary and syntax |
| **Abstract** | "Justice is important," "freedom." | Abstract nouns and symbols |

### Present reality

If you want to express an action happening now, language offers *is* + verb-*ing*:

> "The cat is sleeping."

### Past reality

If an event already happened, you cannot go back in time to infer it — so language offers past tense:

> "The cat was sleeping."

### Imagined reality

For imagination — things that do not make sense in the current world — language still offers words:

> "The flying banana," "the cat jumping over the moon."

### Abstract reality

For complex abstract concepts — justice, freedom — language offers abstract words, and people attach symbols:

- **Freedom:** a flag, the Statue of Liberty
- **Peace:** a white bird

These are hard to symbolize, but people find their own ways.

## 13. Ambiguity: The Limits of Symbols

Language has limits. Symbols can be ambiguous.

### Example 1: "The cat jumped over the wall."

Consider possible interpretations:

- **A:** The cat jumped across the wall, from one side to the other.
- **B:** The cat jumped above the wall.
- **C:** The cat jumped onto the wall (less precise).
- **D:** Some combination of the above.

The word **"over"** has a **range of interpretations**: across, above, from one side to another. The word **"jump"** does not encode the trajectory or shape of the cat's flight. The information is not fully encoded in "over."

This is why multiple whiteboard drawings can all be argued as correct. **The sentence underspecifies the scene.**

### Example 2: "John saw Mary with a telescope."

Two meanings:

1. Mary has a telescope; John saw Mary (who was carrying it).
2. John used a telescope to see Mary.

The culprit is **"with"** — it can attach to either the seeing event or to Mary.

### The lesson

These are examples of **ambiguity**. Language symbols can be problematic. You need more context — discourse information, coherence, pragmatic knowledge — to articulate the intended idea.

A useful exercise: ask native speakers of different backgrounds to interpret these sentences. Interpretations vary, and that variation is itself informative.

## 14. The Interactive Function: Language as a Two-Body Problem

The second approach to implementing communication is **interactive**.

Here, language is used to:

- Get your ideas across
- Persuade the other person
- Convince someone
- Get tasks done
- Give reasons so others believe you

### The transmitter–receiver model

```
SPEAKER / TRANSMITTER  →  message  →  HEARER / LISTENER / DECODER
```

The speaker **encodes**; the hearer **decodes**. Successful communication requires both.

### The trade-off

When you speak, you make choices:

- Which sentences to use
- Which words to use

These choices involve a **trade-off**:

- **Conserve energy** — speaking costs effort: jaw movement, voice, breath.
- **Maximize understanding** — you care about your audience understanding you.

You can phrase things in a way that helps you conserve energy, or in a way that gets your ideas across more fully. Often you balance both.

### Why it is a two-body problem

> **Interactive communication is not a one-body problem. It is a two-body problem.**

You must take care of not just yourself (the speaker) but also your audience (the hearer). This is a crucial difference from the symbolic function, which is about the form–meaning pairing itself.

## 15. Can Machines Truly Understand Language?

> **Question:** When can we confidently say that a machine has almost completely understood our conceptualization — that it can interpret our expression with 100% accuracy?

> **Answer:** This is a very important and very active area of research.

### Why it is hard

Language is a **form of behavior**. Behavior is a function of two things:

1. **Human cognition**
2. **Environment**

Both factors influence language behavior, which makes it a very complex problem.

Machines do not have cognitive functionality. To make machines understand language, we need to **computationally model cognitive functionality** — build cognitive models that represent humans more closely.

### The current problem with AI

A major issue in the current AI world:

> We do not really understand how language functions, yet we feed language into models and expect human-like language comprehension and production.

The fix:

1. **Spend time with language itself** — study language functionality and language use.
2. **Understand the mechanisms** — form–meaning pairing, concept-building, pragmatics, ambiguity.
3. **Then build better algorithms** whose outputs resonate more like humans.

This is the takeaway that should guide everything that follows — including **Natural Language Understanding (NLU)**.

## 16. Summary: The Nine Principles

1. **Language is symbolic.** Concepts are labeled with forms.
2. **Forms must pair with meaning.** This is form–meaning pairing.
3. **Language organizes thought.** Messy room → tidy room.
4. **Language reconstructs projected reality.** Inner world ↔ outer world.
5. **Perception ≠ concept ≠ meaning.** Signals → concept → meaning.
6. **Grammar encodes realities.** Present, past, imagined, abstract.
7. **Pragmatics handles context and ambiguity.** *Bank*, *over*, *with a telescope*.
8. **Interaction is a two-body problem.** Speaker and hearer; energy vs. understanding.
9. **Machines need cognitive models.** NLU is active research; do not just feed data and assume understanding.

### One-sentence takeaway

> **Language is a symbolic, context-dependent reconstruction protocol: a mind encodes concepts into forms, another mind decodes those forms into meaning — and ambiguity and context are handled by pragmatics and interaction.**
